💡 Why testimonials matter more than you think

🔴 CriticalOther people's words are ten times more convincing than yours

When Alice says "The Stillwater Letters is a thoughtful, faith-filled newsletter for women," people think: "Of course she'd say that." But when a reader says "This newsletter is the first thing I open every Tuesday morning — it feels like a letter from a friend," people think: "I want that too." Social proof bypasses scepticism in a way self-promotion never can. Every testimonial you collect is a quiet sales tool that works forever.

🟡 ImportantTestimonials you don't capture are lost — log them the moment they arrive

Kind words come in at unexpected moments — a reply to Tuesday's newsletter, a comment on Instagram, a message from someone you met at church. If you don't write them down immediately, they're gone. Open the Command Centre, click "+ Add Testimonial," and log it on the spot. Even a rough paraphrase is better than a lost quote.

🟢 GoalUse them everywhere — your sign-up page, newsletters, social posts, and membership invitations

A testimonial sitting in the hub but never being used is a wasted opportunity. The "Used" checkbox in the Testimonials section is there to help you track which quotes have been deployed — and which are still waiting. Aim to feature at least one testimonial per month in your marketing.

👣 How to log and use testimonials

When a kind message arrives — log it immediately

Don't save it for later. Open the Command Centre on your phone, click "+ Add Testimonial," and paste or type the quote. It takes 60 seconds. Messages left in your inbox to "deal with later" get buried — and lost.

💡 Bookmark the Command Centre on your phone's home screen so it's always one tap away when a message arrives.

Choose the right source so you know where your best quotes come from

Email ReplySomeone replied directly to your newsletter
Instagram DMA direct message on Instagram
Facebook CommentA comment on a Facebook post
In PersonSomething said face-to-face — paraphrase it clearly
💡 Over time, you'll notice patterns. If most of your best quotes come from email replies, that tells you your newsletter is doing its job. If they come from in-person conversations, you may have untapped reach in your local community.

Use testimonials actively — don't let them sit unused

Here's where to put them:

  • Sign-up page — 2–3 quotes directly beneath your sign-up form
  • Newsletter footer — one quote at the bottom of each issue ("Here's what readers are saying…")
  • Instagram posts — screenshot or type out a quote as a graphic
  • Hands of Hope invitation — include a quote from a current paid member
  • Pinterest graphics — a powerful quote as a pin drives curiosity and clicks

Tick "Used" when you feature a quote somewhere

This keeps your testimonials organised. When you're planning a social post and need a quote, you can quickly scan for ones you haven't featured yet — the unticked rows are your ready-to-use pool.

🙋 How to ask for testimonials — without it feeling awkward

When someone sends you a meaningful reply to your newsletter, respond warmly — and at the end, add one simple line:

"Thank you so much for sharing that with me — it genuinely means the world. Would it be okay if I shared your words with others? I can use just your first name, or keep it anonymous — whatever you prefer."

Almost everyone says yes. People who write kind things are usually happy to have them shared. The asking is the hardest part — and this phrasing makes it feel like a gift, not a request.

Once a month or once a quarter, add a short paragraph to the bottom of your newsletter:

"If The Stillwater Letters has meant something to you this year, I'd love to hear about it. A simple reply — even one line — helps me keep writing. And if you're happy for me to share your words, it helps others find their way here too. Just hit reply. Thank you."

This approach collects testimonials passively — people reply when they feel moved to, which means the quotes you receive are genuine and timely.

The best testimonials do one or more of these things:

  • Describe a specific feeling or moment: "I read this on a Wednesday morning when I was really struggling, and it helped me breathe."
  • Name a specific newsletter or piece of content: "The letter about Psalm 22 is the most honest thing I've read all year."
  • Include the outcome: "I forwarded it to three friends and all three subscribed."
  • Use the kind of language your ideal reader would recognise: "It doesn't feel preachy — it feels like a conversation with someone who gets it."

A short, specific quote beats a long, general one every time. "I look forward to this every week" is more convincing than a paragraph of praise — it's concrete and easy to believe.
